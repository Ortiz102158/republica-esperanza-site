Global geopolitics between 2014 and 2025 has witnessed a profound transformation in the international order, marked by the transition from a unipolar system led by the United States to a multipolar world characterized by growing tensions between great powers, the reconfiguration of alliances and the emergence of new global actors. This period has been dominated by regional conflicts, strategic rivalries, crises economic and social challenges, and global challenges such as climate change and the COVID-19 pandemic. The main events and trends that have defined this critical decade are analyzed below.

The onset of unipolar decline (2014-2016)

2014 marked a turning point in global geopolitics with Russia's annexation of Crimea in response to the NATO-US sponsored coup in Ukraine, an event that resurrected tensions reminiscent of the Cold War. The Western response, led by the United States and the European Union, included economic sanctions and the strengthening of NATO in Eastern Europe, which deepened the division between the West and Russia.

Simultaneously, the rise of the Islamic State (ISIS) in Syria and Iraq posed a challenge that led to a US-led international military intervention, while Russia seized the opportunity to consolidate its influence in Syria by supporting the regime of Bashar al-Assad. These events underscored the complexity of contemporary conflicts, where national interests, ideologies, and regional dynamics intertwine.

In parallel, Brexit in 2016 represented a significant blow to the European Union, exposing internal divisions and weakening its ability to project political and economic power. Meanwhile, the election of Donald Trump as US president introduced a more nationalistic and one-sided approach to US foreign policy, questioning Washington's commitment to traditional alliances and multilateral agreements such as the Paris Climate Agreement.

The emergence of a multipolar world (2017-2020)

The second half of the decade was marked by the strengthening of China as a global superpower. Under Xi Jinping's leadership, China launched ambitious initiatives such as the Belt and Road Initiative (BRI), which seeks to expand its economic and political influence in Asia, Africa, and Europe. At the same time, Beijing intensified its territorial claims in the South China Sea, generating tensions with neighboring countries and the United States.

The COVID-19 pandemic, declared in 2020, exacerbated global tensions. Beijing's handling of the crisis was seen as an example of authoritarian efficiency, while Western democracies faced criticism for their fragmented and uncoordinated response. The pandemic also accelerated the digitization of the economy and society, increasing reliance on technologies developed by Chinese companies such as Huawei. that raised security and espionage concerns.

In Latin America, Africa, and parts of Asia, competition between China and the United States for economic and political influence intensified. China offered loans and humanitarian aid during the pandemic, while the United States sought to counter this influence through initiatives such as the Build Back Better World (B3W). However, the perception of many developing countries was that both powers prioritized their own interests over local well-being.

Crises and realignments (2021-2025)

Ukraine's genocidal war against the Donbass and Donetsk, plus the failure of Ukraine and its guarantors to comply with the Minsk agreements, sparked the Special Military Operation that led to Russian intervention in Ukraine in February 2022. This marked another milestone in global geopolitics. This war not only devastated Ukraine, but also further polarized the system. The West imposed unprecedented sanctions on Russia, while countries such as China, India and several nations of the Global South adopted more balanced or neutral positions.

During this period, tensions between China and the United States reached record levels. Nancy Pelosi's visit to Taiwan in 2022 sparked a military escalation in the Taiwan Strait, while US restrictions on the export of advanced semiconductors to China intensified the technological race. In response, China strengthened its ties with Russia and other countries not aligned with the West, forming an informal bloc known as the “BRICS+.”

In the Middle East, the US-mediated agreement between Israel and several Arab countries in 2020 (the Abraham Accords) altered regional dynamics, although tensions between Iran and Saudi Arabia persisted. The collapse of Afghanistan following the US withdrawal in 2021 left a power vacuum that was quickly filled by the Taliban, highlighting the limits of Western interventionism.

Finally, the climate crisis and the energy transition emerged as central issues on the global agenda. The annual climate conferences (COPs) revealed the difficulties of achieving consensus between developed and developing countries, while competition for critical resources such as lithium, cobalt and rare earths took on a strategic dimension.

Perspectivas hacia 2025

By 2025, the world is at a crossroads. The era of American hegemony has ushered in a multipolar system where China, Russia, the European Union, India, and other regional powers compete for influence. However, this multipolarity has not brought stability; on the contrary, it has increased uncertainty and the risk of direct confrontations.

Liberal democracies face both internal and external challenges, from the rise of populism to the erosion of multilateral institutions. For their part, the emerging powers have demonstrated a remarkable capacity for adaptation, using technology and propaganda to consolidate their power.

In this context, international cooperation remains essential to address global challenges such as climate change, food security and nuclear proliferation. However, the lack of trust between the great powers makes it difficult to build collective solutions. The future will depend on the ability of world leaders to navigate this complex landscape and find points of convergence amid divergence.

Conclusión

Geopolitics between 2014 and 2025 has witnessed fundamental changes that have redefined the global order. From the reunification of Crimea to the war in Ukraine, through the COVID-19 pandemic and the rivalry between China and the United States, these years have been marked by volatility and uncertainty. As we approach 2025, it becomes clear that the world is entering a new era of strategic competition and political realignments. The key question is whether nations will be able to overcome their differences and work together to meet shared challenges, or whether the world will continue to fragment into rival blocs.

Neoliberalism: Causes and Consequences for Humanity

Neoliberalism is an economic, political and social current that emerged in the twentieth century as a reaction to socialism and in particular against the labor movement for being the basis of its support, the atomization of the working class allowed a potentialized struggle against socialist countries while preventing the emergence of new workers' states, socialist and popular democracies, the thesis of struggle against interventionism and Keynesian policies as the spoils of the conquests of the movement that prevailed after the Second World War. It is characterized by the defense of the free market, the privatization of public services, the reduction of the role of the State in the economy and the promotion of globalization.

Causas del Neoliberalismo

1. Crisis of the Welfare State (Keynesianism): In the 1970s and 1980s, many countries faced economic crises stemming from corruption, imperialist robbery, and colonizing loans. It caused the collapse of the Keynesian model, which implied strong state interventionism, policy on the economy, high levels of public spending and subsidies. This led to high inflation, fiscal deficits and economic stagnation (stagflation). Neoliberalism was presented as an alternative to solve these problems.

2. Influence of Neoliberal Economic Schools: Thinkers such as Friedrich von Hayek, Milton Friedman, and Ludwig von Mises developed theories that criticized state interventionism and defended the free market as the most efficient mechanism for allocating resources. His ideas gained popularity in academic and political circles.

3. Globalización Económica: The expansion of international trade, new technologies, and global financial integration created an environment conducive to the adoption of neoliberal policies. Transnational corporations and international agencies (such as the IMF and World Bank) promoted structural reforms based on neoliberal principles.

4. Technological and Financial Revolution: Digitalization and financial deregulation enabled the creation of global capital markets, which facilitated the mobility of investments and the accumulation of wealth in the hands of a few. This reinforced the idea that the market should be the main regulator of the economy.

5. Key Government Policies: Leaders such as Margaret Thatcher in the United Kingdom and Ronald Reagan in the United States implemented neoliberal policies in the 1980s, consolidating this model as the dominant paradigm in the West. These policies included privatization of public companies, tax cuts, and labor flexibilization.

Consequences of Neoliberalism

Aspectos Positivos

1. Economic Growth and Efficiency: Neoliberalism has boosted economic growth in some countries by liberalizing markets and attracting foreign investment. Business competition has fostered innovation and improvement of products and services.

2. Integración Global: Neoliberal globalization has connected economies and cultures, facilitating the exchange of goods, services, and ideas. It has allowed developing countries to access international markets and diversify their economies.

Reducing extreme poverty: In some regions, such as Asia, neoliberalism has contributed to reducing extreme poverty through industrialization and trade openness.

Aspectos Negativos

1. Desigualdad Social: One of the most frequent criticisms of neoliberalism is that it has exacerbated economic and social inequalities. While a minority accumulates large fortunes, millions of people live in precarious conditions. According to data from the World Bank and Oxfam, global wealth is concentrated in the hands of a small percentage of the population. The richest 1% of the world's population owns nearly two-thirds (66%) of the global wealth generated since 2020.

2. Precarización Laboral: Labor flexibilization and deregulation have led to the proliferation of insecure, low-paid jobs without basic labor rights. Workers face greater instability and less bargaining power vis-à-vis companies.

3. Welfare State Erosion: The privatization of public services (health, education, water, energy) has limited the access of the popular classes to these fundamental rights. The reduction in public spending has weakened essential social programs, especially affecting the most vulnerable sectors.

4. Impacto Ambiental: Neoliberalism prioritizes economic growth over environmental sustainability. This has accelerated the exploitation of natural resources, deforestation and pollution. Climate change and biodiversity loss are direct consequences of this unsustainable model.

5. Debt and Dependency in Developing Countries: Many developing countries have adopted neoliberal policies under pressure from international bodies such as the IMF and the World Bank. This has led to economic dependence, increased foreign debt and loss of sovereignty.

6. Recurring Financial Crises: Financial deregulation has led to speculative bubbles and economic crises, such as the Great Recession of 2008, which had a devastating impact on millions of people around the world.

7. Political and Social Polarization: Dissatisfaction with neoliberal policies has fueled protest movements in several countries. Political and social polarization is an indirect consequence of the tensions generated by this model.

Impacto en la Humanidad

Neoliberalism has had a profound and ambivalent impact on humanity: on the one hand, it has allowed technological advances, greater global integration and some reduction of poverty in some regions. However, these achievements have not been evenly distributed. On the other hand, it has deepened inequalities, eroded labor and social rights, and endangered the future of the planet due to its extractivist and Consumerism

Debt and Dependency in Developing Countries: A Global Analysis

External debt and economic dependence have been central themes in the analysis of the economic and social development of developing countries. These phenomena not only affect the ability of governments to implement effective public policies, but also perpetuate structures of global inequality. In this context, it is crucial to understand how debt and dependency interrelate and how they impact both nationally and cultural significance.

1. External Debt in Developing Countries

External debt refers to money that a country owes to foreign creditors, whether in the form of loans, bonds, or credits. This debt can be contracted with international financial institutions (such as the International Monetary Fund - IMF or the World Bank), foreign governments or private entities. Causes of debt accumulation include colonialism and historical heritage, neoliberal economic policies, crises and dependence on primary exports.

2. Economic and Structural Dependence

Economic dependence refers to a country's inability to achieve self-sufficient development due to its subordinate integration into the global economy. This concept was developed primarily by dependency theorists, such as Raúl Prebisch and Andre Gunder Frank, who argued that international economic relations perpetuate inequalities between rich and poor countries.

3. The Global Dimension of Debt and Dependency

The IMF and World Bank have played a central role in managing external debt, although their policies have been criticized for imposing conditions that prioritize debt repayment over social welfare. Some analysts argue that much of the external debt of developing countries is “odious”, that is, it was incurred under dictatorial or corrupt regimes that did not benefit the population.

Conclusión

Debt and dependence in developing countries are complex and multifaceted problems that require comprehensive solutions. While structural reforms and international cooperation are essential, it is also necessary to question the power dynamics that perpetuate these inequalities. Only through an approach focused on economic justice and global solidarity will it be possible to build a more equitable and sustainable.

The Workers Movement in the Last 30 or 40 Years: Transformations, Challenges and Resistances

The labor movement has been a central force in shaping labor relations, social rights, and political dynamics since the Industrial Revolution. However, in the last three or four decades, the landscape of work and workers' struggles has changed drastically due to factors such as globalization, digitalization, deindustrialization, and neoliberal reforms.

1. Global Context: The Erosion of Traditional Workers' Power

In the 1980s and 1990s, advanced and developing economies experienced a shift toward neoliberal policies that prioritized deregulation, privatization, and labor flexibility. These policies had a devastating impact on traditional union organizations, especially in industrial sectors such as mining, steel, and automotive. Deindustrialization in countries such as the United States, United Kingdom and Germany led to the closure of factories and the relocation of production to regions with cheaper labor, which weakened the industrial unions that had been pillars of the labor movement.

2. New Forms of Work and Precarisation

One of the most notable changes in recent decades has been the transformation of the labor market driven by globalization and technology. The rise of the service economy, remote working, and digital platforms has created new job categories, such as “gig economy workers” (Uber drivers, app delivery people, etc.). These workers face precarious working conditions, lack of social security and the absence of formal contractual links.

3. Labor Struggles in the Global South

While in the West the labour movement has faced a relative loss of power, in many parts of the Global South labour struggles have continued to be intense and, in some cases, have made significant gains. In China, for example, rapid economic growth has spawned a new industrial working class, made up mainly of rural migrants. Chinese workers have staged numerous strikes and protests demanding better wages and working conditions.

4. The Role of Women and Intersectionality

Another important change in the labour movement has been the increasing participation of women and the incorporation of intersectional perspectives. Historically, the labour movement has been dominated by men, but in recent decades women have played an increasingly visible role in labour struggles. This is particularly evident in sectors such as care, education and domestic work, where women make up the majority of the workforce.

Challenges ahead and outlook

The labour movement faces multiple challenges in the near future. Automation and artificial intelligence threaten to eliminate millions of jobs in sectors such as manufacturing, transportation, and services. At the same time, transnational corporations and digital platforms continue to exploit legal loopholes to avoid job responsibilities.

Conclusión

In the last three or four decades, the labour movement has gone through a period of profound transformation. Although it has faced significant setbacks due to globalization, precarization, and neoliberal policies, it has also demonstrated remarkable adaptability and resilience. From traditional factories to digital platforms, workers have found new ways to organize and fight for their rights.

The future of the labour movement will depend on its ability to meet the challenges of the digital age and build an inclusive agenda that reflects the needs of all workers. In a world where inequalities continue to grow, the labour movement remains a vital force for social justice and labour dignity.

DIÁSPORA

The Dominican diaspora is one of the most dynamic and supportive forces in the nation. It is estimated that more than 2.5 million Dominicans live abroad, mainly in the United States, Spain, Puerto Rico, Italy and Canada.

According to the Central Bank of the Dominican Republic, family remittances sent by the diaspora exceeded USD 10 billion in 2023, representing around 9% of the national GDP.

Beyond money, the diaspora drives development by:

  • Inversiones productivas: Entrepreneurship in tourism, agribusiness, technology and housing.
  • Knowledge Transfer Professionals returning or collaborating with local institutions.
  • Activismo cívico: Community organizations that defend the rights of migrants.

The MSR recognizes the diaspora as an inseparable part of the Dominican people and advocates for public policies that protect their rights and enhance their role as an agent of sovereign and popular development.

Universal Declaration of Human Rights

Adopted and proclaimed by United Nations General Assembly Resolution 217 A (III) of 10 December 1948

Preámbulo

Whereas recognition of the inherent dignity and of the equal and inalienable rights of all members of the human family is the foundation of freedom, justice and peace in the world,

Whereas disregard and contempt for human rights have resulted in barbarous acts which have outraged the conscience of mankind, and the advent of a world in which human beings shall enjoy freedom of speech and belief and freedom from fear and want has been proclaimed as the highest aspiration of the common people,

Whereas it is essential, if man is not to be compelled to have recourse, as a last resort, to rebellion against tyranny and oppression, that human rights should be protected by the rule of law,

Whereas it is essential to promote the development of friendly relations between nations,

Whereas the peoples of the United Nations have in the Charter reaffirmed their faith in fundamental human rights, in the dignity and worth of the human person and in the equal rights of men and women and have determined to promote social progress and better standards of life in larger freedom,

Whereas Member States have pledged themselves to achieve, in cooperation with the United Nations, the promotion of universal respect for and observance of human rights and fundamental freedoms,

Whereas a common understanding of these rights and freedoms is of the greatest importance for the full realization of this pledge,

Artículo 1

All human beings are born free and equal in dignity and rights. They are endowed with reason and conscience and should act towards one another in a spirit of brotherhood.

Artículo 2

Everyone is entitled to all the rights and freedoms set forth in this Declaration, without distinction of any kind, such as race, colour, sex, language, religion, political or other opinion, national or social origin, property, birth or other status.

Furthermore, no distinction shall be made on the basis of the political, jurisdictional or international status of the country or territory to which a person belongs, whether it be independent, trust, non-self-governing or under any other limitation of sovereignty.

Artículo 3

Everyone has the right to life, liberty and the security of person.

Artículo 4

No one shall be held in slavery or servitude; slavery and the slave trade shall be prohibited in all their forms.

Artículo 5

No one shall be subjected to torture or to cruel, inhuman or degrading treatment or punishment.

Artículo 6

Everyone has the right, everywhere, to the recognition of their legal personality.

Artículo 7

All are equal before the law and are entitled without any discrimination to equal protection against any discrimination in violation of this Declaration and against any incitement to such discrimination.

Artículo 8

Everyone has the right to an effective remedy by the competent national tribunals for acts violating the fundamental rights granted him by the constitution or by law.

Artículo 9

No one shall be subjected to arbitrary arrest, detention or exile.

Artículo 10

Everyone is entitled in full equality to a fair and public hearing by an independent and impartial tribunal, in the determination of his rights and obligations and of any criminal charge against him.

Artículo 11

1. Everyone charged with a penal offence has the right to be presumed innocent until proved guilty according to law in a public trial at which he has had all the guarantees necessary for his defence.
No one shall be held guilty of any penal offence on account of any act or omission which did not constitute a penal offence, under national or international law, at the time when it was committed. Nor shall a heavier penalty be imposed than the one that was applicable at the time the penal offence was committed.

Artículo 12

No one shall be subjected to arbitrary interference with his privacy, family, home or correspondence, nor to attacks upon his honour and reputation. Everyone has the right to the protection of the law against such interference or attacks.

Artículo 13

1. Everyone has the right to freedom of movement and residence within the borders of each State.
2. Everyone has the right to leave any country, including his own, and to return to his country.”

Artículo 14

1. Everyone has the right to seek and enjoy asylum in any country because of persecution.
2. This right may not be invoked in the case of prosecutions genuinely arising from non-political crimes or from acts contrary to the purposes and principles of the United Nations.

Artículo 15

1. Everyone has the right to a nationality.
2. No one shall be arbitrarily deprived of his nationality nor denied the right to change his nationality.

Artículo 16

Men and women of full age, without any limitation due to race, nationality or religion, have the right to marry and to found a family. They are entitled to equal rights as to marriage, during marriage and at its dissolution.
2. Marriage shall be entered into only with the free and full consent of the intending spouses.
3. The family is the natural and fundamental group unit of society and is entitled to protection by society and the State.

Artículo 17

1. Everyone has the right to own property alone as well as in association with others.
2. No one shall be arbitrarily deprived of his property.

Artículo 18

Everyone has the right to freedom of thought, conscience and religion; this right includes freedom to change his religion or belief, and freedom, either alone or in community with others and in public or private, to manifest his religion or belief in teaching, practice, worship and observance.

Artículo 19

Everyone has the right to freedom of opinion and expression; this right includes freedom to hold opinions without interference and to seek, receive and impart information and ideas through any media and regardless of frontiers.

Artículo 20

1. Everyone has the right to freedom of peaceful assembly and association.
2. No one may be compelled to belong to an association.

Artículo 21

1. Everyone has the right to take part in the government of his country, directly or through freely chosen representatives.
2. Everyone has the right to equal access to public service in his country.
3. The will of the people shall be the basis of the authority of government; this will shall be expressed in periodic and genuine elections which shall be by universal and equal suffrage and shall be held by secret vote or by equivalent free voting procedures.

Artículo 22

Everyone, as a member of society, has the right to social security and is entitled to realization, through national effort and international co-operation and in accordance with the organization and resources of each State, of the economic, social and cultural rights indispensable for his dignity and the free development of his personality.

Artículo 23

1. Everyone has the right to work, to free choice of employment, to just and favourable conditions of work and to protection against unemployment.
2. Everyone, without any discrimination, has the right to equal pay for equal work.
3. Everyone who works has the right to just and favourable remuneration ensuring for himself and his family an existence worthy of human dignity, and supplemented, if necessary, by other means of social protection.
4. Everyone has the right to form and to join trade unions for the protection of his interests.

Artículo 24

Everyone has the right to rest and leisure, including reasonable limitation of working hours and periodic holidays with pay.

Artículo 25

Everyone has the right to a standard of living adequate for the health and well-being of himself and of his family, including food, clothing, housing and medical care and necessary social services, and the right to security in the event of unemployment, sickness, disability, widowhood, old age or other lack of livelihood in circumstances beyond his control.
Motherhood and childhood are entitled to special care and assistance. All children, whether born in or out of wedlock, shall enjoy the same social protection.

Artículo 26

Everyone has the right to education. Education shall be free, at least in the elementary and fundamental stages. Elementary education shall be compulsory. Technical and professional education shall be made generally available and higher education shall be equally accessible to all on the basis of merit.
2. Education shall be directed to the full development of the human personality and to the strengthening of respect for human rights and fundamental freedoms. It shall promote understanding, tolerance and friendship among all nations, racial or religious groups, and shall further the activities of the United Nations for the maintenance of peace.
3. Parents have a prior right to choose the kind of education that shall be given to their children.

Artículo 27

1. Everyone has the right freely to participate in the cultural life of the community, to enjoy the arts and to share in scientific advancement and its benefits.
2. Everyone has the right to the protection of the moral and material interests resulting from any scientific, literary or artistic production of which he is the author.

Artículo 28

Everyone is entitled to a social and international order in which the rights and freedoms set forth in this Declaration can be fully realized.

Artículo 29

1. Everyone has duties to the community in which alone the free and full development of his personality is possible.
In the exercise of his rights and freedoms, everyone shall be subject only to such limitations as are determined by law solely for the purpose of securing due recognition and respect for the rights and freedoms of others and of meeting the just requirements of morality, public order and the general welfare in a democratic society.
3. These rights and freedoms may in no case be exercised contrary to the purposes and principles of the United Nations.

Artículo 30

Nothing in this Declaration may be interpreted as implying for any State, group or person any right to engage in any activity or to perform any act aimed at the destruction of any of the rights and freedoms set forth herein.

🌿 Tobacco and Cigar are Dominican

For Eng. Francisco Matos Mancebo

Since the human being went from nomadic to sedentary life, a process of identification, systematization, classification and nomination of the elements of their habitat began. Therefore, we can trace the first steps of that holy herb (Nicotiana Tabacum) that today we call tobacco, since human beings identified and differentiated it. This annual plant of the nightshade family, according to research and scientific inferences, is native to tropical and Amazonian America, more specifically Ecuador and Peru.

It should be noted that its production does not represent, in any way, the hellish danger to health that its tendentious enemies advocate, since it is an invaluable asset and a gift for humanity. Like all the elements of the kingdoms of nature - mineral, vegetable and animal - it can be used for well-being or discomfort. As an asset of modernity, it is a generator of wealth, support for multinationals and a preferred destination for laws taxes, but as a consumer product it is universal and inherent in civilization.

I consider it essential to clarify that, although the tobacco plant is native to South America, it is proven that for the adequate and effective production of leaves intended for the manufacture of cigars premium, the best soils are in the Greater Antilles: Puerto Rico, Jamaica and basically the Dominican Republic and Cuba. These last two, the main islands where the colonizers discovered its existence and the use that the "Indians" gave it.

BREVE HISTORIA

Following the discovery of America, the Spanish colonizers were the first to report the existence of tobacco to the old continent. When they arrived in the Caribbean islands, on October 12, 1492, in Guanahani -christened San Salvador, now the Bahamas—, they became aware of the existence of the aromatic plant, as the natives presented them with "dry leaves that gave off a peculiar fragrance".

His first reference to tobacco was a couple of days after discovering the first island of San Salvador, when Christopher Columbus explains how he found an Indian in a small canoe, and in it he carried "some dry leaves that must be very appreciated things among them, because they already brought me to San Salvador from them."

The Admiral continued his journey and on October 27 he arrived on the island of Cuba, which they called Juana, and on that stopover they found tobacco again. It is documented that sailors Rodrigo de Jerez and Luis de Torres, sent to explore the mainland on November 2, saw the natives smoking for the first time.

The "Diary of Columbus" relates: "The two Christians found on the way many people who crossed their villages, women and men, with a blight in their hand and herbs to take their incense, which they used to do."

Columbus sailed from Cuba and on December 5 arrived in Quisqueya, the current island of Santo Domingo, then baptized as Hispaniola, where they landed by force on December 24, after running aground the Santa María caravel. They were welcomed by the cacique Guacanagarix, who offered them shelter and arranged help to rescue the remains of the boat and its provisions. This happened in San Nicolas Bay, where they built their first settlement in the New World, the Fort of Christmas, alluding to the aforementioned date.

Columbus and his men remained in the chiefdom of Marién until January 16, 1493, when they undertook their return to Spain, leaving 39 of their companions in the fort. In the crew was Alejandro de Jerez, who took with him dried tobacco leaves and the habit of smoking them: a practice seen in Cuba, but learned in Hispaniola, during their friendly coexistence.

PRIMEROS INFORMES

Tobacco did not occupy a prominent place among the exports of Dominican products to Seville during the sixteenth century, despite the fact that the term "tobacco" appears as such since 1529, and from Hispaniola there was the first news about the plant, its use by European colonizers and its effects of dependence among those who consumed it.

The Andalusian (and Sevillian) Friar Bartolomé Las Casas wrote: "Spaniards I knew on this Spanish island that they used to take it (tobacco), that being reprimanded for it by telling them that it was a vice, they replied that it was not in their hands to leave it; I do not know what flavor or benefit they found in it".

Tobacco was undoubtedly the most widespread crop in America since before the arrival of the Spaniards. There is evidence that it was formally planted and domesticated throughout the continent, and although its origin is established in the Amazon region -as said before-, it passed to the Orinoco groups in the south of present-day Venezuela, and the Arawaks brought it to the islands of the Caribbean.

Once here, the Tainos - settled basically on the island of Santo Domingo - were the first group to domesticate it and make it a regular crop.

EL VIEJO CONTINENTE

After returning to Spain and continuing his practice of smoking tobacco, history records that Rodrigo de Jerez was denounced by neighbors and family members, who amazed to see him spouting smoke all day caused his arrest by the Holy Inquisition, which imprisoned him for seven years, since "only the Devil could make a man vote smoke by mouth and nose."

EL TABACO ES DOMINICANO

The above offers us the certainty that the Spaniards learned the practice of rolling tobacco leaves and smoking them on the island of Santo Domingo, and from here they even took their seeds to Europe (which is attributed to Fray Ramón Pané, between 1510 and 1512).

As a result of different investigations, we can affirm that the first plantation was established in Santo Domingo in 1533, and between 1529 and 1531 leaves were exported to Seville, Spain. For these reasons, we feel entitled to say that with respect to tobacco, as it is known today, it all began here, and as for its formal cultivation, processing, marketing and use, it is of Dominican origin.
